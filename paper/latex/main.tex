{Optimized Topic Modeling of AI Research Involving Nepali Authors:\\
Tracking Thematic Evolution and Trends in Nepal (2015--2025)}
{%
Artificial intelligence research produced in low resource countries is rarely characterised
systematically, and topic-model comparisons are typically run on large, clean corpora unlike the
regional literature most institutions actually hold. We construct a corpus of 1{,}074 scholarly
AI/ML papers (2015-2025) with at least one Nepal affiliated author and compare four topic models:
standard Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA), a genetic algorithm optimised LDA searching
$(K,\alpha,\beta)$ against a multi objective fitness of coherence, diversity and stability, a

An independent review of the 39 generated topic labels corroborates this from a different angle:
only 1 of GA-LDA's 7 labels was accepted unchanged, against 9 of BERTopic's 14. Both model families independently identify generative AI in education as the
dominant rising theme. We conclude that optimising coherence metrics optimises neither
interpretability nor anything the evolutionary machinery uniquely provides.%

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:t1-intro}

AI research output has grown faster than any individual can track by reading. Scientometric topic
modelling addresses this by inferring themes from publication corpora, and Latent Dirichlet
Allocation (LDA)~\cite{blei2003} remains the standard tool because its topics are directly
interpretable as word distributions. LDA models each document as a mixture over latent topics and
each topic as a distribution over words, with Dirichlet priors $\alpha$ and $\beta$ governing how
concentrated those mixtures are; the number of topics $K$ is not learned and must be chosen.
Applied to scientific corpora since Griffiths and Steyvers~\cite{griffiths2004}, it remains the
default instrument for mapping a research field~\cite{yu2023}.

Two gaps motivate this study. First, the scientometric literature concentrates on global corpora;
research produced in low-resource countries is characterised anecdotally if at all, and Nepal's AI
output has no systematic account. Second, methodological comparisons of classical against neural
topic models are typically conducted on large, homogeneous corpora precisely the conditions
under which LDA is best behaved. Whether an evolutionary optimised LDA can match a modern embedding
model on a \emph{small, heterogeneous, regional} corpus is a different and less studied question.

\subsection{Related work}

Agrawal, Fu and Menzies~\cite{agrawal2018} show that LDA's instability under random initialisation
is severe and that metaheuristic search over its hyperparameters improves both stability and
coherence. Their argument motivates the GA-optimised model evaluated here directly, and we test
their claim on a corpus type they did not study. Yu and Xiang~\cite{yu2023} apply standard LDA to global AI
literature, establishing the thematic mapping baseline this work extends temporally and regionally.
Embedding based topic modelling with BERTopic~\cite{grootendorst2022} has been applied at scale to
domain literature~\cite{agri2025}, reporting more specific topics than LDA but without the
document level topic distributions LDA provides. BERTopic replaces probabilistic inference with a
pipeline of sentence embeddings~\cite{reimers2019}, dimensionality reduction by
UMAP~\cite{mcinnes2018} and density based clustering by HDBSCAN~\cite{campello2013}; the last of
these declines to assign outliers, which is the source of the unclustered documents reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results}. Haq et al.~\cite{haq2019} apply text mining to 200
cloud computing papers, producing a single static snapshot the limitation this study addresses
by tracking themes across four time windows.

Evaluation of topic models is itself contested. Perplexity, the natural probabilistic criterion,
correlates poorly with human judgement of topic quality~\cite{chang2009}, which motivated
coherence measures built on word co-occurrence statistics~\cite{newman2010,mimno2011}. We adopt
the $C_v$ formulation of R\"{o}der et al.~\cite{roder2015} as the primary measure and topic
diversity following Dieng et al.~\cite{dieng2020}. Our interpretability results support Chang et
al.'s original warning strongly.

\subsection{Contribution}

The contribution is the combination rather than any single component: a regional scientometric
application, a GA-optimised LDA with a multi objective fitness, and a four way evaluation that
includes a budget matched control and an interpretability assessment alongside coherence. We do not
claim to invent an algorithm or to be first to optimise LDA with a genetic algorithm. Two of our
findings run against the model we proposed: random search \textbf{outperforms} the genetic
algorithm at equal budget, and the interpretability ranking \textbf{inverts} the coherence ranking.

\section{Problem and Dataset}

\subsection{Research questions}

\textbf{RQ1:} what themes characterise AI research involving Nepal affiliated authors, 2015-2025?
\textbf{RQ2:} how have those themes shifted across the decade?
\textbf{RQ3:} does GA-optimised LDA outperform grid-searched LDA, and does it outperform a
budget matched random search?
\textbf{RQ4:} how does it compare with BERTopic?

\subsection{Corpus construction}

Papers were retrieved through the OpenAlex works API, filtered server side on
\texttt{authorships.countries:np} together with AI and computer vision topic subfields and a
publication year range, then supplemented by a keyword pass to recover applied AI filed under other
primary topics.

\textbf{Choice of source.} OpenAlex~\cite{priem2022} was selected over the alternatives for a
methodological reason: it exposes a structured \texttt{authorships.countries} filter, which is a
reliable instrument for the inclusion rule ``has a Nepal affiliated author''. The principal
alternative, Semantic Scholar, cannot return author affiliations from its bulk search endpoint,
which would have forced fragile institution name string matching --- a materially weaker
instrument for exactly the criterion that defines this corpus.

The cost of that choice is coverage. NepJOL, Nepal's national journal platform, exposes no public
API and is therefore \emph{absent} from this corpus, which is likely to under represent
Nepali language and locally published work. This is a genuine limitation and
Section~\ref{sec:selp} returns to it.

\subsection{Inclusion and filtering}

Inclusion required at least one author with a Nepal based institutional affiliation, and AI method
vocabulary in the title or abstract. From 1{,}693 retrieved records the analysis corpus was built by
the documented funnel in Table~\ref{tab:funnel}.

by reading model output rather than by auditing the corpus, which is itself an argument for
inspecting topics before trusting metrics.

\subsection{Corpus properties}

The corpus contains 1{,}074 documents across 564 distinct venues, with a median document length of
212 words. \textbf{93.5\% are journal or conference publications}; 3.4\% are preprint-server records
and 3.1\% carry no venue, so we describe the corpus as \emph{scholarly AI literature} rather than
claiming it is wholly peer reviewed. There are no duplicate titles or identifiers.

The temporal distribution is heavily skewed (Fig.~\ref{fig:years}): 8, 93, 299 and 674 documents
across 2015-17, 2018-20, 2021-23 and 2024-25 respectively. This skew constrains the analysis
design and qualifies every temporal claim.

A 30 record manual audit found \textbf{zero incorrect Nepal affiliations}, and 99.8\% of records
carry an identifiable Nepali institution. The audit also found approximately 7\% of sampled papers
are law or policy papers \emph{about} AI rather than papers \emph{using} AI methods --- a
borderline category we retain, since scientometric studies of AI conventionally include AI policy
and ethics.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Preprocessing}

Documents are the concatenation of title and abstract. Cleaning applies NFKC normalisation and
strips leading ``Abstract'' prefixes (71 documents), copyright tails, URLs, DOIs and parenthesised
acronym glosses. That last step matters: authors write ``artificial intelligence (AI)'', and phrase
detection consequently produced \emph{both} \texttt{artificial\_intelligence} (191 documents) and
\texttt{artificial\_intelligence\_ai} (160) for one concept. Stripping the gloss consolidated it to
265 while leaving standalone \texttt{ai} (253) intact.

For LDA, text is lowercased, tokenised, stop word filtered, lemmatised (WordNet, noun then verb),
and bigram/trigram phrases are detected. \textbf{Technical acronyms are protected} from the
minimum length filter and the lemmatiser, so \texttt{ai}, \texttt{ml}, \texttt{nlp}, \texttt{cnn}
and \texttt{lstm} survive; 21 of 29 reach the final dictionary.

Domain stop words were selected by \textbf{measurement, not intuition}. Generic research process
vocabulary was scored by document frequency (DF), and the 77 terms at $\text{DF} \ge 5\%$ removed
\emph{challenge} 32.5\%, \emph{enhance} 30.4\%, \emph{improve} 29.0\%. The standard
\texttt{no\_above=0.5} filter caught only one term (``model'', 59.8\%); everything in the 18-33\%
band would otherwise have appeared in every topic. Two candidates, \texttt{level} and
\texttt{value}, were deliberately \textbf{retained}: this corpus contains hydrology and health
research where ``water level'' and ``$p$-value'' are content. The final dictionary holds
\textbf{3{,}018 terms}, with no empty documents.

\textbf{BERTopic consumes a separate branch} cleaned natural sentences, not the LDA token
stream. A sentence transformer depends on word order and function words; supplying it lemmatised
bags of words would have invalidated the comparison.

\subsection{Standard LDA (baseline)}

LDA is fitted by online variational Bayes~\cite{hoffman2010} as implemented in
Gensim~\cite{rehurek2010}, with $K$ swept over $\{5,10,\dots,40\}$ and selected by $C_v$. The
comparison is therefore GA-search versus grid search, not GA versus an arbitrary $K$.
Figure~\ref{fig:coherence-k} shows the full sweep: $C_v$ peaks at $K{=}10$, which is
therefore the baseline reported in Table~\ref{tab:comparison}. Topic diversity is not
monotonic in $K$ and was not used for baseline selection, only $C_v$ was, to keep the
baseline a clean single criterion grid search that the GA and random search are then
compared against.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figCoherenceVsK.png}

\{5,10,\dots,40\}$. $K{=}10$ is selected because it maximises $C_v$; this is the
grid-searched baseline that GA-LDA and random search LDA are compared against in
Section~\ref{sec:results}.}
\label{fig:coherence-k}
\end{figure}

\subsection{GA-optimised LDA (proposed)}

A genetic algorithm~\cite{holland1975} searches $(K,\alpha,\eta)$ directly.\footnote{The topic word
prior is written $\beta$ in the LDA literature and \texttt{eta} in Gensim. They are the same
parameter.}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Encoding:} real valued, not binary. $\alpha$ and $\eta$ span two orders of magnitude;
a bit string would impose an arbitrary discretisation on the dimensions the search exists to
explore finely.
\item \textbf{Initialisation:} log uniform on $\alpha$ and $\eta$. Uniform sampling over
$[0.01, 1.0]$ places $\sim$90\% of draws above 0.1 and barely explores the sparse prior region where
topic models typically operate.
\item \textbf{Selection:} tournament, size 3. Fitness occupies a narrow band ($\sim$0.40-0.53), so
fitness proportionate selection would apply near equal pressure to good and bad genomes.
\item \textbf{Crossover:} BLX-$\alpha$, rate 0.7. Plain interval crossover can only interpolate
between parents, so the population contracts toward its own mean.
\item \textbf{Mutation:} Gaussian, $\sigma = 0.15 \times$ gene range, rate 0.05. Scaling $\sigma$
per gene keeps mutation comparable across parameters whose ranges differ by orders of magnitude.
\item \textbf{Elitism:} 2, so the best solution cannot be lost to crossover.
\end{itemize}

The fitness function is a weighted sum,
\begin{equation}
F = 0.6\,\widehat{C_v} + 0.2\,D + 0.2\,S,
\end{equation}
where $\widehat{C_v}$ is $C_v$ min-max normalised over $[0.30, 0.70]$ so all three terms share a
scale, $D$ is topic diversity and $S$ is stability. Population 20, 15 generations. Repeated genomes
are memoised, reducing the 900 nominal model fits to 549 actual ones.

\subsection{Random search control}

The GA was compared against a grid of 8 $K$-values with fixed $\alpha$ and $\eta$, while itself
searching a continuous three-dimensional space. Any advantage could therefore be explained either by
the evolutionary mechanism or simply by searching more finely. Random search is a strong baseline
for exactly this kind of low-dimensional hyperparameter problem~\cite{bergstra2012}, so to separate
the two explanations we ran random search over the identical space with the identical fitness
function, stability seeds and LDA settings, at a budget \textbf{matched on unique fitness
evaluations} (183, taken from the GA's actual cost). Both strategies draw candidates from the same
log uniform sampler. The two runs differ in exactly one respect: how candidates are proposed.

\subsection{Stability}

Stability is defined here as follows: fit the configuration
under $m$ seeds; for each pair of runs, match topics one-to-one by maximising total Jaccard overlap
of top $N$ word sets (Hungarian assignment); take the mean matched overlap, averaged over pairs.
Hungarian matching is essential because topic indices are arbitrary two runs recovering
identical topics in different orders would otherwise score near zero.

\subsection{Temporal analysis}
\label{sec:temporal-method}

Re-fitting the model separately within each time window is not viable here: the 2015-17 window
holds only 8 documents. We instead fit one global model and report mean
document topic proportions per window, normalised \emph{within} window so window size cancels. The
2015-17 window is reported but excluded from trend fitting, and per window $n$ is printed on every
table and figure.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

All experiments use seed 42 and single-process coherence evaluation. Coherence is computed as $C_v$
(primary), $c_{\text{npmi}}$ and $u_{\text{mass}}$ over top 10 words; topic diversity and mean
pairwise Jaccard over top-25; stability over three seeds; and perplexity. Reporting three coherence
measures is deliberate: the GA and the random control both optimise $C_v$, so quoting $C_v$ alone as
evidence of success would be circular.

BERTopic uses \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} embeddings, UMAP ($n_{\text{neighbors}}{=}15$,
$n_{\text{components}}{=}5$, cosine, fixed \texttt{random\_state}) and HDBSCAN
(\texttt{min\_cluster\_size}$\,{=}\,$15, EOM), with the \emph{same} stop-word list used for LDA so the
comparison measures modelling rather than preprocessing.

Interpretability was assessed by a panel of \textbf{three independent raters}, each scoring all 39
topics 1-5 on whether the top-10 words name one identifiable research area. The panel was blind:
every rater received only shuffled word lists and the rubric no model names, no draft labels,
and no sight of another rater's output. A fourth reviewer separately audited all 39 generated topic
labels, returning accept, revise or reject.

\textbf{The raters are automated (large language model) judges rather than human raters.} Their
independence is genuine separate contexts, blind inputs so the agreement statistic is
meaningful, but they are not humans and the reader should weigh the result accordingly. Agreement
is computed with Krippendorff's $\alpha$~\cite{krippendorff2004} using an ordinal difference
function, chosen over Fleiss' $\kappa$ because the scale is ordinal.

\textbf{RQ3  GA-LDA versus baseline, and versus a matched control.} Against the grid searched
baseline, GA-LDA improves every computed metric (Table~\ref{tab:comparison}). The $C_v$ gain (+10.2\%) is partly expected since
$C_v$ is the optimisation target; more meaningful are $c_{\text{npmi}}$, $u_{\text{mass}}$ and
perplexity, \textbf{none of which the GA saw}. $c_{\text{npmi}}$ crossing from negative to positive
is substantive, since negative $c_{\text{npmi}}$ indicates top words close to statistical
independence.

\textbf{But the random search control reverses the conclusion.} At an identical budget of 183 unique
evaluations, random sampling of the same space found a better solution: fitness 0.5282 vs.\ 0.5196,
$C_v$ 0.5030 vs.\ 0.4940, $c_{\text{npmi}}$ 0.0245 vs.\ 0.0112, perplexity 165.9 vs.\ 167.8, and
interpretability 2.88 vs.\ 2.05. The GA wins only $u_{\text{mass}}$, a measure neither strategy
optimised. \textbf{We therefore cannot claim that evolutionary search outperforms alternatives on
this problem.} The supportable claim is narrower: searching $(K,\alpha,\beta)$ finely beats an
8-point grid, and the search strategy is not what matters.

One nuance qualifies that. Only \textbf{1 of 183} random draws beat the GA's final solution, so the
GA reliably reaches a good region while random search needed 183 attempts to find one better point.
On this evidence the GA behaves as a \emph{variance-reduction} mechanism rather than an
optimum-finding one valuable if a single run must be trusted, but not the premise on which it
was adopted.

\textbf{RQ4  versus BERTopic.} No model dominates. BERTopic takes $C_v$ and stability by large
margins but is worst on $c_{\text{npmi}}$, $u_{\text{mass}}$ and diversity, and leaves 17.1\% of
documents unclustered. Two caveats qualify its coherence figures: its topics derive from 83\% of the
corpus, and only 66.4\% of its topic words exist in the shared reference dictionary, the remainder
being dropped before scoring. Its stability advantage (0.842 vs.\ 0.338) has a clear mechanism:
embeddings are deterministic, so only UMAP and HDBSCAN vary across seeds, whereas LDA's variational
inference is re initialised entirely.

\subsection{Convergence}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figGaConvergence.png}
\caption{GA convergence. Best fitness is flat from generation 8, and the population mean nearly
reaches it, the population collapsed onto one genome and roughly half the compute produced no
improvement.}
\label{fig:convergence}
\end{figure}

Best fitness rose from 0.4865 to 0.5196 by generation 8 and was flat thereafter; the population mean
rose from 0.4140 to 0.5130, nearly reaching the best (Fig.~\ref{fig:convergence}). This is the most
likely explanation for the control result: from generation 8 onward the GA was resampling a narrow
neighbourhood while random search continued to explore the whole space, so the GA effectively spent
half its budget on a smaller random search. A higher mutation rate, a diversity-maintenance
mechanism, or restarts would be the obvious remedies.

\subsection{Interpretability inverts the coherence ranking}

The three raters agreed strongly: Krippendorff's $\alpha = 0.979$ (ordinal), 93.2\% exact
agreement and \textbf{100\% agreement within one point}. Panel means:
\textbf{BERTopic 4.10} $>$ Random-Search LDA 2.88 $>$ standard LDA 2.57 $>$ \textbf{GA-LDA 2.05}.

The independent label review corroborates this through a different task. Of the 39 machine-generated
labels, 20 were accepted unchanged, 12 revised and 7 rejected as unsupported by the words. The
per model split is stark: BERTopic \textbf{9 of 14} accepted with \textbf{zero} rejections, against
GA-LDA \textbf{1 of 7} accepted with 3 rejected. Two evaluations of different kinds scoring
topics, and auditing labels place the same model last.

\textbf{This inverts the coherence ranking, and it is the paper's most consequential finding.} The
GA converged to $K{=}7$ because fewer, broader topics scored well on a fitness of coherence,
diversity and stability. Those topics are correspondingly generic: GA-LDA topic 5 is
\emph{accuracy, data, performance, classification, prediction, image, detection, dataset} 
entirely evaluation vocabulary, describing a method rather than a subject. BERTopic's topics name
domains directly: \emph{drug, molecular, inhibitors, screening, binding}; \emph{concrete,
compressive strength}; \emph{stock, price, market, trading}. \textbf{Optimising coherence metrics
did not optimise interpretability; on this corpus it worked against it.}

learning, transformer models and generative AI''. The generative-AI half is strongly supported. The
deep learning half is \textbf{not}: deep learning topics \emph{decline} as a share. Our
interpretation is saturation deep learning became the default method rather than a distinct
theme but that is interpretation, not measurement. Shares are relative: a declining share does
not mean less absolute research, since the corpus grows sharply.

\section{Social, Ethical, Legal and Professional Considerations}
\label{sec:selp}

\textbf{Scientometric profiling of identifiable people.} This corpus names institutions and, through
author lists, individual researchers in a small national research community where a topic can map to
a handful of people. Aggregate thematic findings are legitimate; ranking named institutions or
individuals would not be, and we do not. We report themes, never productivity by person or
institution.

\textbf{Data provenance and licensing.} OpenAlex data is CC0 and its API was used within its
documented polite pool conventions, with rate limiting and a contact email. Only metadata and
abstracts were retrieved; no paywalled full texts were accessed.

\textbf{Equity in representation.} English language, Western indexed databases systematically
under represent scholarship published locally and in Nepali. NepJOL, the national journal platform,
is \emph{absent} from this corpus for the technical reason that it has no public API. Our picture of
``AI research in Nepal'' is therefore a picture of \emph{internationally indexed} AI research
involving Nepali authors, and that gap is likely to fall on exactly the locally oriented work a
study of Nepal should most want to capture.

\textbf{Declared use of language models.} A language model drafted candidate topic labels from
top word lists and produced the interpretability ratings reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}.
This is an instrument used within the method, and it is documented reproducibly: the exact prompt
template, the model used, and the review status are all recorded alongside the results. It is
distinct from using a language model to write the submission, which we have not done. Because the
same family of system both fitted the models and rated their output, we treat those ratings as
provisional pending human review.

\textbf{Reporting of unfavourable results.} The model this study proposed lost to a trivial control
and scored lowest on interpretability. Both outcomes are reported in full, in the same table as the
results that favoured it, because a comparison that suppresses its own negative findings is not a
comparison.

\section{Discussion and Conclusions}
\label{sec:t1-conclusion}

\textbf{Metric choice can reverse a conclusion.} GA-LDA wins four of nine numeric rows and loses
interpretability decisively. Optimising coherence drove $K$ down and produced broader, less
meaningful topics. Practitioners selecting a topic model on coherence alone should expect this
failure mode.

\textbf{Hyperparameter search works; the evolutionary machinery is not what makes it work.} GA-LDA
beats the grid searched baseline on every metric, including three it did not optimise. But a random
search at matched budget beat the GA in turn, on fitness, $C_v$, $c_{\text{npmi}}$, perplexity and
interpretability. The supportable conclusion is that LDA on this corpus is sensitive to
$(K,\alpha,\beta)$ and rewards any fine search, and that the evolutionary mechanism contributed
nothing detectable beyond that. Without the control, the comparison alone would have supported
``GA-optimised LDA outperforms standard LDA'' true as stated, and misleading as an argument for
genetic algorithms.

\textbf{Embeddings are markedly more stable on a small corpus} (0.842 vs.\ 0.338). For a
1{,}074 document corpus this is a strong practical argument for BERTopic though 17.1\% of
documents go unmodelled, which is unacceptable where document level assignment is required, as in
our temporal analysis.

\textbf{Limitations.} (i) Absolute stability for every LDA variant is low (0.25-0.34): even the
best model recovers about a third of the same structure across seeds, which qualifies every
topic level claim here. (ii) The 2015-17 window holds 8 documents; its trend line is indicative
only. (iii) NepJOL's absence biases coverage against locally published work. (iv) Interpretability
rests on automated raters. Their agreement is high ($\alpha = 0.979$), but high agreement among
models sharing a training distribution is weaker evidence than agreement among independent people.
(v) The GA was run once with one parameter setting; a fairer test would tune its mutation rate and
population size, and repeat both searches over multiple seeds to compare distributions rather than
single runs.

\textbf{Conclusion.} On a small regional corpus, fine hyperparameter search improves LDA measurably
over grid search, but our genetic algorithm achieved nothing that random sampling at the same cost
did not. Meanwhile the model that best serves the actual research purpose reading what a field
is about is BERTopic, which the coherence metrics ranked ambiguously and the interpretability
assessment ranked first. Both of our headline methodological expectations were wrong in the same
direction: the metrics we optimised were not measuring what we cared about. Both model families
nevertheless agree that generative AI in education is the fastest rising theme in Nepal affiliated
AI research, and that the field is predominantly applied rather than methodological.
